## 03.01 — Examine an ultimate question without manufacturing certainty

**Goal.** Compare religious, spiritual, philosophical and naturalistic accounts of ultimate questions, then state a reasoned position or uncertainty that represents alternatives fairly.

**How this represents the competency.** The six-question map covers God, consciousness, soul, freedom, providence and transcendence; one comparison receives deeper examination. A reasoned position is an inquiry result, not a proof, conversion, or comprehensive survey of traditions.

**Before starting.** Use paper, dictation or a private note. No religious affiliation or previous philosophy study is required. Start with the supplied question: does the feeling that a helpful coincidence was meant to happen justify believing it was providential? Use the constructed viewpoints below as starting positions, not descriptions of every believer or nonbeliever.

**Time and effort.** Three sessions of about 20 minutes, with an optional later return to an unresolved question.

### 1. Separate six questions that are easily collapsed together

Make six short entries and consider a contrast for each: God as a personal creator versus impersonal ultimate reality or no divinity; consciousness as physically explained versus having an irreducible experiential aspect; soul as a persisting immaterial self versus a changing embodied pattern; freedom as acting from reasons within causes versus requiring another kind of agency; providence as guiding intention versus events without such direction; transcendence as contact with ultimate reality versus going beyond ordinary self-concern. These are constructed contrasts, not exhaustive accounts or labels for entire traditions. For each, state what the disagreement is and a present view or honest question. Separate experience, explanation, moral commitment and claims about reality; feeling awe alone does not answer all six.

Observable checks:

- All six questions include a considered contrast.
- Experience and interpretation are distinguished.
- Present uncertainty is recorded rather than concealed.
- The focused question is stated in ordinary language.

### 2. Give competing accounts their strongest reasons

Apply four constructed accounts to the coincidence: a personal God may guide events; an impersonal spiritual order may make the event meaningful; a philosophical skeptic asks which inference is justified; a naturalistic account examines ordinary causes and attention to memorable events. Write a reason each proponent might offer and a serious difficulty for each. Ask what observation would distinguish the accounts, and which disagreement may remain metaphysical rather than experimentally decidable. Read Enchiridion 31 through the supplied reference for one actual providential account, and keep that author's claim separate from your reconstruction.

Observable checks:

- Four distinct accounts are represented fairly.
- Each account has a reason and a difficulty.
- A possible discriminator or its absence is stated.
- A source-backed claim is distinguished from constructed viewpoints.

### 3. State what you believe and how you could revise it

Write or speak a short conclusion with five parts: my current view or suspension of judgment; my strongest reason; the strongest remaining objection; what could change my view; and one practical consequence that does not require others to agree. Revisit the other five entries and note whether the conclusion actually changes them. In the coincidence case, you might appreciate the help and thank the person while leaving divine intention unresolved. Explain an alternative without sarcasm before defending your own conclusion.

Observable checks:

- The conclusion includes a reason and an unresolved objection.
- A revision condition is explicit.
- Practical conduct does not require manufactured agreement.
- Implications for the other questions are checked.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use the supplied coincidence instead of personal religious experiences. Definitions can be read aloud; a drawing can separate observation from explanation. A chosen faith commitment and principled nonbelief are both possible outcomes. Leave questions open or use N/A if this pathway is consciously unchosen.

**Review.** Can someone who disagrees recognize their view in your account, and can you say why you hold or withhold your conclusion without pretending proof?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The inquiry separated a meaningful experience from an explanation, represented four alternatives, and retained uncertainty about providence while identifying a reasoned next question.
- **Mixed:** The competing accounts were fair, but the supposed discriminator would not actually separate them and the conclusion stayed provisional.
- **Contradictory:** The intensity of a feeling was presented as decisive proof, or a competing worldview was reduced to stupidity or moral inferiority.
- **Inconclusive:** Definitions were copied without comparing reasons or stating a position, objection or principled uncertainty.

**Reference support:**

- [Enchiridion: sections 1, 17 and 31](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html) — These selected passages connect judgments and choice with assigned roles and a providential account of the gods. Historical philosophical positions, not established scientific findings. The archive credits Elizabeth Carter; this exercise uses the displayed edition without reproducing its wording. No claim that all Stoics agree or that ancient prescriptions about suffering and obedience should govern present conduct.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.02 — Study one wisdom tradition beyond its slogans

**Goal.** Build an account of one tradition from primary passages, historical setting, an exemplar, practice, internal differences and a present community's own account.

**How this represents the competency.** This is a small study unit, not completion of a tradition. The default examines ancient Stoic texts and a contemporary organization; one organization never represents all adherents. Study does not require adopting the beliefs.

**Before starting.** Use the linked Enchiridion sections 1, 17 and 31; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations Book One, opening entries through Rusticus; and Modern Stoicism's About Us page. The numbered passages and prompts below define the unit. Paper or spoken notes suffice. Another chosen tradition needs an accessible primary passage, historical context, an exemplar and a self-described living community before substitution; otherwise use this default.

**Time and effort.** Three study sessions of about 25 minutes over a week; allow more time or split the reading if needed.

### 1. Read a primary passage inside its setting

Read the three Enchiridion sections and the opening Meditations entries. Record author, work and section so you can find each again. Contrast the handbook's counsel about choice, assigned roles and providential gods with Marcus's acknowledgments of teachers and family. Locate the ancient texts before the contemporary organization in a simple timeline; use the archive's dating as an approximate guide. In your own words, explain what kind of life these selected passages commend and what conception of the world supports it. Mark an unfamiliar term instead of silently translating it into a modern slogan.

Observable checks:

- Primary passages have retrievable locations.
- Ancient and contemporary settings are distinguished.
- A way of life and its underlying view are connected.
- An unfamiliar term or assumption is investigated.

### 2. Test an interpretation through a modest practice

Use Marcus's account of Rusticus as the selected exemplar: careful reading and accepting correction matter more than displaying learned words. Re-read one passage and correct your first interpretation using its actual wording. Then try one ordinary action inspired by the texts, such as preparing a clear request while leaving the other person's reply to them. Record what you did and whether it served the stated purpose. Do not adopt ancient claims about illness, enslavement or obedience as instructions to dismiss suffering or tolerate abuse; explain that interpretive limit explicitly.

Observable checks:

- An exemplar is connected to a concrete teaching.
- The first interpretation is checked against the passage.
- An ordinary practice receives an actual attempt.
- An ethical or historical limitation remains visible.

### 3. Explain the tradition with its live differences intact

Read Modern Stoicism's own description of its origins, activities and range of religious views. Compare that contemporary range with Enchiridion 31's providential account: identify the interpretive question rather than declaring them identical. Prepare a two-minute explanation with one primary teaching, its historical setting, an exemplar, your trial, this difference, and the organization's present activities. End by correcting a caricature, such as treating Stoicism as simply hiding every feeling, using the selected passages. Name what further reading or a voluntary community conversation would need to check.

Observable checks:

- A present community is described from its own account.
- A real interpretive difference is preserved.
- The explanation connects all six study elements.
- A caricature is corrected without claiming total expertise.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use text to speech or divide the named sections into short readings. If source access fails, use the included summaries only for orientation and leave primary-text and community-study checks unmarked until the sources are available; do not invent a substitute quotation. No visit, membership, donation or assent is needed.

**Review.** Could a beginner distinguish this account from a motivational slogan, and which claim would you most want a knowledgeable practitioner or scholar to correct?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The learner connected passage, setting, exemplar, a request they actually made, a disagreement over providence and a current organization's self-description.
- **Mixed:** The primary passages and practice were examined, but a contemporary community's account was unavailable and that part remained unverified.
- **Contradictory:** One modern adaptation was called the whole tradition, or an ancient prescription was used to excuse mistreatment without examination.
- **Inconclusive:** An attractive quotation was collected without its context, a practical attempt, or examination of interpretive differences.

**Reference support:**

- [Enchiridion: sections 1, 17 and 31](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html) — These selected passages connect judgments and choice with assigned roles and a providential account of the gods. Historical philosophical positions, not established scientific findings. The archive credits Elizabeth Carter; this exercise uses the displayed edition without reproducing its wording. No claim that all Stoics agree or that ancient prescriptions about suffering and obedience should govern present conduct.
- [Meditations: Book One, opening entries through Rusticus](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.1.one.html) — Marcus identifies lessons received from family and teachers; the Rusticus entry includes careful reading and a willingness to recognize a need for correction. A particular author's account of exemplars, not evidence that every claim in the text is accurate or that the product's study and practice unit develops virtue.
- [About Modern Stoicism](https://modernstoicism.com/about-us/) — This organization describes a project originating in 2012 and welcomes different religious views and interpretations within its contemporary Stoic activities. The organization's own description is a primary account of its stated approach, not independent verification of its practices, a representation of all Stoics or endorsement of its research claims.

## 03.03 — Build a short contemplative practice and examine its effects

**Goal.** Establish a freely chosen prayer, reading, silence or attention practice and examine whether it supports honesty, attention, gratitude, resolve or peace in ordinary conduct.

**How this represents the competency.** Five brief occasions provide an initial pattern, not reliable clinical or spiritual efficacy. The practice may be useful, neutral or unwelcome; concentration, calm and religious feeling are not scores of worth.

**Before starting.** Choose a familiar safe place and an ordinary daily cue. The usable default is an eyes-open three-minute pause: notice the room, attend to one ordinary sound or object, then name one honest next action. Prayer, sacred reading, examen or quiet recitation may replace the middle minute if already suitable to your tradition. No special posture, breath control, equipment or belief is required.

**Time and effort.** About 15 minutes to prepare, five three-minute occasions during a week, and a 15-minute review.

### 1. Define a practice small enough to repeat honestly

Write the cue, the three-minute sequence and one conduct marker: for example, notice that you do not know before making a claim, thank a helper specifically, or return attention to the next task. Choose one of honesty, attention, gratitude, resolve or peace as the focus, while checking that the others are not being undermined. State how to stop comfortably. Rehearse the sequence once; record an interrupted rehearsal as interrupted rather than stretching it until it looks successful.

Observable checks:

- A cue and exact short sequence are defined.
- One observable conduct marker is selected.
- A comfortable stopping option is available.
- One rehearsal is recorded accurately.

### 2. Repeat without requiring a special internal state

On five available occasions, follow the sequence or record why it was missed. After each, privately note the setting, whether you actually practiced, what happened to the chosen marker in the next ordinary activity, and any discomfort or distraction. If attention wanders, gently return once; wandering is information rather than misconduct. Do not increase intensity to force an effect. If the practice becomes distressing, stop, return to an ordinary activity and record that it was unsuitable.

Observable checks:

- Five opportunities are accounted for without inventing attempts.
- Actual practice and missed opportunities are separated.
- Conduct after practice is observed.
- Discomfort and distractions remain in the record.

### 3. Choose a sustainable next step from the whole pattern

Compare the actual occasions, including neutral and difficult ones. Ask whether any apparent change could instead reflect sleep, workload, company or expectations. Look separately at honest acknowledgment, sustained attention, appreciation, follow-through and ease; do not require every effect. Keep, shorten, change or stop the sequence for a stated reason. A useful result might be admitting uncertainty twice even though you never felt peaceful. Describe the pattern as a small personal observation, not proof that this practice works for everyone.

Observable checks:

- Helpful neutral and difficult occasions are compared.
- At least one competing explanation is considered.
- A specific keep adapt or stop decision follows.
- The conclusion stays within the observed pattern.

**Adaptations and limits.** Attend to touch, sound or an ordinary task using a comfortable posture; keep eyes open if preferred. Use a familiar prayer only by choice. No fasting, sleep deprivation, breath manipulation, isolation or altered-state induction belongs to this exercise. Stop an upsetting practice and seek suitable support if distress persists.

**Review.** What happened to your chosen conduct marker, what did the practice cost, and is the next version justified by your actual experience?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** Four brief attempts were recorded, one was missed, and two later conversations included honest acknowledgment of uncertainty; no general effectiveness claim was made.
- **Mixed:** The pause sometimes helped attention but also felt burdensome, so the participant shortened it and retained the uncertainty.
- **Contradictory:** A participant extended an upsetting session to force calm, concealed missed occasions or treated distraction as spiritual failure.
- **Inconclusive:** A preferred routine was planned but no actual occasions or later conduct were observed.

**Reference support:**

- [Meditation and Mindfulness: Effectiveness and Safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety) — Meditation can involve negative experiences; health concerns should not be managed by replacing appropriate care with meditation. Supports a cautious stop-and-support boundary only. It does not establish safety or effectiveness of this product's short pause, classify a spiritual experience, or validate individual outcomes.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.04 — Turn reverence into attentive and responsible conduct

**Goal.** Recognize what merits reverent attention and respond with care while retaining conscience, factual judgment and respect for people who understand sacredness differently.

**How this represents the competency.** Life, nature, persons, ritual, truth, beauty and what is held holy are possible objects of reverence, not a required belief list. One chosen encounter tests sincere attention and conduct; it cannot establish the truth of a sacred claim.

**Before starting.** Use something safely accessible: a plant, the sky through a window, a piece of music you may play, an inherited object, or a freely chosen familiar ritual. The default is observing an ordinary plant without touching or removing it. No special journey, purchase, emotional response or religious assent is needed.

**Time and effort.** Three short sessions of about 15 minutes, including one ordinary encounter and a later care action.

### 1. Name what deserves attention and what respect requires

Consider the seven possibilities in the scope and choose one that matters to you. Describe what you value about this particular example rather than calling everything sacred. For the plant, consider its living processes and dependence on its surroundings. State appropriate conduct: observe without damage, follow access rules, and leave other people's interpretation free. If the object involves a person or tradition, distinguish your admiration from permission to photograph, touch, imitate or demand disclosure.

Observable checks:

- A particular object of reverence and reason are identified.
- The chosen pathway is distinguished from required belief.
- Appropriate conduct is specified.
- Other people retain consent and interpretive freedom.

### 2. Attend sincerely while keeping observation and meaning separate

Spend five comfortable minutes with the chosen example. Notice three concrete details and one question you cannot answer. Describe any awe, gratitude, indifference or discomfort without forcing it. Then add your interpretation separately: for instance, the branching leaf pattern prompted care for living things, but did not prove a supernatural account. If a claim conflicts with evidence or conscience, record the disagreement while behaving respectfully; sincerity does not require saying something you believe false.

Observable checks:

- Three details come from an actual encounter.
- A remaining question is acknowledged.
- Feeling and explanation are recorded separately.
- Respectful conduct preserves factual and conscientious judgment.

### 3. Carry the attention into one proportionate act of care

Within two days, take a small action that follows from the value you named: care correctly for your own plant, avoid damaging a shared place, listen attentively to a willing person, or return a meaningful object to its proper place. For the default, leave the plant undisturbed and improve care of something you are responsible for. Review whether the action served that good or mainly displayed your own sensitivity. State how someone who holds nothing holy in the same way still deserves equal respect.

Observable checks:

- A proportionate care action is actually taken.
- The action relates to the stated value.
- Care and self-display are distinguished.
- Respect extends to people outside the chosen pathway.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use familiar music, a described object, a tactile item you own or an accessible view. No contact with sacred objects, restricted practices, wildlife or unwilling people is required. If nothing feels reverent, honest attention can be observed without claiming awe; this pathway may also be unchosen.

**Review.** Did attention change how you treated something, and could you retain that care without forcing your interpretation on others?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The participant noticed details of a plant, left its meaning open, and changed how they cared for a living thing in their responsibility.
- **Mixed:** The encounter felt ordinary rather than moving, but the participant still acted carefully and reported the lack of awe.
- **Contradictory:** A moving experience was used to overrule another person's conscience, claim unsupported truth or damage a place while seeking a dramatic photograph.
- **Inconclusive:** A value was named without an actual attentive encounter or any related conduct.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.05 — Trace a received good and answer it through conduct

**Goal.** Recognize dependence, gifts, contingency, sacrifice and inheritance in an ordinary good, then express gratitude through appropriate care, remembrance or generosity.

**How this represents the competency.** Gratitude concerns a specific received good, not a duty to feel grateful for harm. Recognizing help does not erase your work, create unlimited debt or require contact with an unsafe benefactor.

**Before starting.** Use a skill someone taught you, a book available to you, a useful tool or an ordinary meal. The default is a learned household skill such as mending a loose button or organizing a cupboard. No purchase, public thanks or religious interpretation is needed. Keep names and personal histories in your private notes.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes to trace a good, one modest response, and a 15-minute review after a later use.

### 1. Trace the good beyond your own contribution

Draw a short chain from the good you now use to people, inherited knowledge, materials and circumstances that made it possible. Mark your own effort too. Identify one gift or unearned advantage, one dependence, one contingent opportunity, one cost someone may have borne and one inheritance worth preserving. Label an unknown cost as unknown rather than inventing a sacrifice. For the learned skill, distinguish a teacher's time from the practice you supplied and the tools that happened to be available.

Observable checks:

- The chain includes personal effort and dependence.
- Gift contingency and inheritance are considered.
- A cost or sacrifice is identified without invention.
- Unknown parts of the chain remain explicit.

### 2. Respond in a way that serves the good

Choose a response from this concrete menu: use a borrowed item carefully and return it; thank a willing recipient for a specific help; maintain a tool you own; teach the skill to someone who wants it; or preserve a short private remembrance of a contribution. State why the response is suitable and what it will cost in time or effort. Carry it out without expecting recognition, access or forgiveness in return. If the benefactor also caused harm, acknowledge the limited good without surrendering a needed boundary.

Observable checks:

- The response fits the particular received good.
- A real response is completed.
- The response does not demand a return favor.
- Gratitude preserves any necessary boundary.

### 3. Let remembrance guide the next ordinary use

When you next use the skill, object or opportunity, recall one part of the chain and make a concrete choice about care, restraint, sharing or preservation. Compare this conduct with your original expression of thanks. Ask whether you erased someone, overstated another person's sacrifice or made gratitude into self-accusation. Retain one truthful acknowledgment and one sustainable future behavior. A quiet act of care is sufficient; warm feelings and public display are not required.

Observable checks:

- A later ordinary use is reviewed.
- Remembrance has a concrete consequence for conduct.
- An omission or overstatement is checked.
- The continuing response remains proportionate.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use spoken recollection or a simple object instead of writing. Choose an impersonal public good or a safe historical contribution when direct relationships are difficult. A private acknowledgment can replace contact; do not give money or labor you cannot reasonably spare.

**Review.** Which received contribution became visible, and what did you actually do differently because of it?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The learner credited a teacher and their own practice, maintained a tool and later shared the skill with someone who had requested help.
- **Mixed:** A specific thanks was offered, but the contribution chain still contained unknowns and no later use had occurred.
- **Contradictory:** Gratitude was used to demand obedience, excuse harm or impose a debt that the recipient had never accepted.
- **Inconclusive:** A gratitude list was written without tracing a particular good or taking any related action.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.06 — Release an uncontrollable outcome while taking your next step

**Goal.** Distinguish choice, influence and uncontrollable conditions, tolerate remaining uncertainty and dependence, and act on a proportionate responsibility without pretending to control the outcome.

**How this represents the competency.** One low-stakes uncertainty practices the relation between acceptance and action. It does not prescribe passivity about injustice or claim that feelings, bodies and circumstances are entirely controllable.

**Before starting.** Use an unanswered nonurgent invitation, a hobby plan affected by weather, or the supplied case: you have invited a friend to a free activity but do not yet know whether they will attend. No serious loss, health concern, emergency or manufactured deprivation is needed.

**Time and effort.** About 15 minutes to prepare, one ordinary action, and two brief reviews within a week.

### 1. Sort choice influence and the outcome you cannot promise

Make three columns. Under choice, put a respectful invitation and your own fallback plan. Under influence, put clear timing and an easy way to decline. Under outside my control, put the other person's availability, preference and eventual answer. Add an uncertainty you must live with and a limit on your time or energy. Identify any appropriate help you depend on. Check whether acceptance is becoming an excuse to omit a responsibility, or whether planning is becoming a claim that you can guarantee the reply.

Observable checks:

- Choice influence and uncontrollable conditions are separated.
- An unresolved uncertainty and personal limit are explicit.
- Relevant dependence or help is acknowledged.
- Both passivity and false control are checked.

### 2. Take the action and stop at the agreed boundary

Make or clarify the ordinary invitation once, with a reasonable decision point and an easy refusal. Prepare a satisfying solo or alternative activity. Until the decision point, return to another ordinary task rather than repeatedly demanding a reply. If new information requires action, respond proportionately; releasing control is not a ban on useful follow-up. Observe what you actually did when the urge to check or pressure appeared, without demanding that the feeling disappear.

Observable checks:

- A proportionate action is actually taken.
- A fallback and decision point are available.
- The other person remains free to choose.
- The response to uncertainty is observed without forcing calm.

### 3. Review your conduct separately from whether you got your way

At the decision point, follow the available plan, then review again a day or two later. State the outcome, your contribution, help or constraints, and what remained outside your control. If the reply was disappointing, name the disappointment without using it as proof that acceptance failed. If it was favorable, do not infer that your attitude caused it. Decide whether to repeat the approach, change the invitation or leave the matter alone, giving a practical reason.

Observable checks:

- The decision point leads to a real next step.
- Conduct and outcome are evaluated separately.
- Remaining emotion and dependence are acknowledged.
- The next decision follows the observed limits.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use a solo weather-dependent plan or the fictional invitation for analysis when no suitable live uncertainty exists. Fictional planning does not demonstrate release during a real wait. Prayer or secular language may frame acceptance; neither replaces necessary care, advocacy or professional support.

**Review.** What did you release, what remained yours to do, and how did the result test that distinction?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The participant invited once, accepted a refusal and used the fallback while remaining disappointed but respectful.
- **Mixed:** The fallback worked, but repeated checking continued and became a specific target for a later attempt.
- **Contradictory:** Acceptance was used to abandon a responsibility, or surrender was demanded from someone facing mistreatment.
- **Inconclusive:** The control columns were completed for a fictional case but no real action or period of uncertainty was observed.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.07 — Contribute to a chosen community while keeping conscience intact

**Goal.** Take part in a freely chosen community's shared practice, service, care and accountability, then examine whether belonging and celebration coexist with boundaries against coercion.

**How this represents the competency.** One community contact and contribution can begin participation; they do not establish a trustworthy institution or lasting belonging. A secular ethical or contemplative community is a legitimate chosen pathway, and declining community participation remains possible.

**Before starting.** Choose an existing community you can approach safely, such as a faith group, meditation circle, philosophical discussion group or mutual-care gathering. The default contribution is helping prepare or restore the space for an open gathering after asking the organizer. Confirm visitor access, an exit route and that participation does not require a donation, confession, private disclosure or agreement you do not hold.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes of preparation, an agreed small contribution of up to 30 minutes, and a 15-minute review.

### 1. Learn how this community practices care and accountability

Use public information or a voluntary brief question to learn the gathering's purpose, a shared practice, how help is requested, how achievements or important occasions are marked, and where concerns can be raised. Ask what a newcomer may join or observe. Write one contribution you can genuinely offer and one limit, such as leaving after the agreed time. Treat an unanswered accountability question as missing information. Pressure for secrecy, isolation or personal access is a reason to pause, not a test of devotion.

Observable checks:

- Shared practice care and celebration have concrete examples.
- An accountability route or its absence is identified.
- Visitor participation and a contribution are agreed.
- A personal limit and exit option are explicit.

### 2. Make a useful contribution without purchasing belonging

Carry out the agreed bounded help, such as setting out permitted materials or restoring the room. Ask how the work should be done and accept an ordinary correction when sound. Join or respectfully observe one permitted shared practice, and notice how participants acknowledge effort or care for someone who needs help. Do not record identifying details. Keep the promised time limit; contribution is not consent to membership, proselytizing, unpaid ongoing work or another person's authority over your life.

Observable checks:

- An agreed contribution is actually made.
- A shared practice is joined or explicitly observed.
- An ordinary correction or care interaction is noticed.
- Time and personal boundaries remain intact.

### 3. Decide whether further participation is warranted

Compare what the group said with what you observed about welcome, care, celebration and accountability. Record one appreciated feature, one concern or unknown, and the response to your stated limit. If appropriate, ask one modest clarifying question or offer factual feedback through the stated route; do not confront a coercive situation. Choose return, a smaller role, another community or no further contact. A warm welcome is not proof of safety, and discomfort with an unfamiliar form is not by itself proof of abuse.

Observable checks:

- Stated values and observed conduct are compared.
- A strength and concern or unknown are retained.
- The treatment of a boundary is reviewed.
- Further participation has a reasoned voluntary decision.

**Adaptations and limits.** A suitable online group may offer a small accessible task. If no safe community is available, use a fictional open reading circle to prepare questions and boundaries; leave live contribution and participation checks unmarked. No travel, donation, doctrinal assent, private interview or contact with an unsafe group is required.

**Review.** Was the contribution useful, could you say no, and what evidence supports the amount of trust you are considering next?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The visitor helped restore a room, observed a shared reading, received a clear answer about concerns and left at the agreed time without pressure.
- **Mixed:** The group was welcoming and the task useful, but its accountability route remained unclear and further commitment was deferred.
- **Contradictory:** Help was treated as consent to escalating demands, or a boundary was framed as disloyalty or spiritual inferiority.
- **Inconclusive:** A group was researched but no agreed contribution or shared practice was experienced.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.08 — Make a sustainable commitment to a good beyond yourself

**Goal.** Choose a freely endorsed commitment to truth, faith, community, craft, a cause or future people and let it make a real, proportionate claim on your time, comfort, resources or status.

**How this represents the competency.** A small completed commitment tests service and follow-through, not discovery of a permanent calling or the moral value of a person. Service must meet a real use rather than treating recipients as props for identity.

**Before starting.** Choose a good you actually endorse and an appropriate recipient or future user. The default is preparing a clear care-and-return instruction card for a shared item, with the responsible person's permission. Use an item you know how to handle safely; do not invent technical instructions. A private reusable guide for your future household can substitute when no shared setting is available, with its narrower benefit stated.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes to choose and agree the work, two 15-minute work periods within a week, and a 15-minute review.

### 1. Turn a large cause into a useful and bounded promise

Name which good calls for your effort and who or what should benefit. Confirm one real need with the responsible person or observable use, such as repeated uncertainty about where a shared item belongs. Define a small deliverable, its recipient, an agreement or authority to act, and a finish date. Identify the real cost: two short blocks that displace optional leisure, a minor inconvenience, or giving another contributor due credit. Protect essential needs and existing commitments; a demanding promise is not automatically a better one.

Observable checks:

- A chosen good has a specific beneficiary or future use.
- A real need and permission are checked.
- A deliverable and finish date are explicit.
- A proportionate cost is acknowledged without neglecting duties.

### 2. Deliver something another person can actually use

Use the first work period to make the instruction card: where the item is kept, how an authorized user checks it, and how it is returned or a problem reported. Verify each statement from known instructions or the responsible person. Use the second period to check readability and completeness with an intended user if willing, then hand it over or place it only where permitted. Credit contributions. Keep promises small enough to finish rather than turning the work into an impressive public project.

Observable checks:

- The promised work receives both actual work periods.
- Content or workmanship is checked against its intended use.
- The deliverable reaches the agreed recipient or place.
- Other contributions and permissions are respected.

### 3. Review service against benefit and actual cost

Ask whether the deliverable was useful, or record that actual use remains unknown. Compare the time and convenience you gave up with the promise and the needs it served. Identify one correction, such as a missing return location, and make it if still within scope. Decide whether this commitment deserves continuation, a different form or a clear ending. A negative review of the deliverable is information about service, not proof that you lack a vocation.

Observable checks:

- Benefit is checked or explicitly remains unknown.
- Actual sacrifice is compared with the promise.
- A needed bounded correction is addressed.
- Continuation or closure has a reason.

**Adaptations and limits.** Offer spoken or large-print instructions, a simple diagram or another requested accessible format. Use a familiar safe item and the authority you actually have. No money, unpaid professional work, recruitment, public credit or irreversible life commitment is required. A private future-use guide does not count as observed benefit to a community.

**Review.** What received something useful, what did the commitment really cost, and what would justify continuing it?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** Two short leisure periods produced a checked return card that an intended user could follow, and a missing location was corrected.
- **Mixed:** The work was delivered and cost real effort, but no later use had occurred, so benefit remained unverified.
- **Contradictory:** The helper ignored the recipient's actual request, sought recognition over usefulness or neglected essential responsibilities to prove devotion.
- **Inconclusive:** A noble cause and intended sacrifice were described without completing an agreed piece of work.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.09 — Use a small ritual to connect remembrance with action

**Goal.** Create or freely adopt a meaningful sequence for a transition, remembrance or shared value, then examine whether its repetition supports memory, identity and practical conduct.

**How this represents the competency.** Beginnings, endings, grief, gratitude, rest, commitment, repentance, celebration and belonging have different purposes. One appropriate ritual is practiced; painful loss or religious obligation need not be introduced to cover every possible use.

**Before starting.** The default is a weekly closing ritual using an ordinary object you own: set it down, name one received good, acknowledge one unfinished matter, state one next action, then begin a short period of rest. No flame, food restriction, purchase, sacred object or public performance is required. Use private notes if speaking aloud is unsuitable.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes to prepare, two five-minute occasions and a 15-minute review.

### 1. Give each part of the sequence a purpose

Choose a real beginning, ending or recurring pause. Briefly distinguish remembering a loss, expressing thanks, making repair, celebrating, belonging and committing: they ask different things of a ritual. For the default, map the object to an ending, thanks to remembrance, the unfinished matter to honesty, the next action to commitment and the final pause to rest. State when the ritual starts and ends and what it does not promise. If borrowing a tradition's form, verify that it is appropriate for you to use; otherwise keep the supplied ordinary sequence.

Observable checks:

- The occasion and purpose are specific.
- Each chosen element has an intelligible meaning.
- Different ritual purposes are not collapsed together.
- Use of objects and any borrowed form is appropriate.

### 2. Perform the sequence with attention and freedom

On the chosen occasion, follow the five-part sequence at a comfortable pace. Name a concrete good and unfinished matter, then choose a small next action such as returning an item or correcting a misunderstanding. Begin the planned rest rather than turning the ceremony into more work. Notice which element helped you remember and which felt empty. If others join, explain the sequence first and make speaking, silence and declining equally available; do not require disclosure of grief, guilt or belief.

Observable checks:

- The defined sequence is actually performed.
- Remembrance refers to a concrete good or event.
- A specific next action is named.
- Rest and any participant choice are respected.

### 3. Check the action and revise the next observance

Before the next occasion, check whether you did the named action and what prevented it if not. Repeat the ritual once, preserving or changing one element for a reason: an object may now remind you to return the borrowed item, while an ornate speech may be removed. Ask what identity or belonging the sequence expresses and whether your conduct supports it. A ritual can be sincere without strong emotion; repeated symbolism does not replace a repair or practical promise.

Observable checks:

- The promised action is checked against what happened.
- A second observance is recorded.
- One retained or revised element has a reason.
- Symbolic meaning is compared with actual conduct.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use a tactile cue, a brief written line or a silent accessible pause. For grief or repentance, choose a manageable event or defer; no forced disclosure, forgiveness or reenactment belongs here. A familiar religious ritual may be used within its community's rules and your conscience.

**Review.** What did the sequence help you remember, and what changed outside the ceremony?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The weekly pause recalled help received, prompted return of a borrowed item and made room for rest; the second observance removed an empty flourish.
- **Mixed:** The sequence felt meaningful, but the next action was missed and the review made that gap explicit.
- **Contradictory:** A ceremony substituted for making a needed repair, pressured others to disclose grief or borrowed a restricted practice without permission.
- **Inconclusive:** A ritual was described beautifully but never performed or linked to an observed action.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.10 — Join a shared sacred practice with understanding and consent

**Goal.** Understand a community's chosen form of worship or remembrance, participate in an eligible and sincere way, and relate the experience to its tradition while preserving conscience and others' freedom.

**How this represents the competency.** One permitted communal form is practiced, not every sacrament, pilgrimage or liturgy. Observation can develop understanding but remains distinct from participation. No one must profess belief, enter a restricted rite or convert to complete a checklist.

**Before starting.** Choose an open, familiar and freely endorsed communal service, recitation, shared silence or remembrance. Ask or read what visitors may do, its meaning, access needs and how to leave. A secular shared remembrance is suitable when that is your pathway. The supplied fallback below is a new shared remembrance with a willing companion, explicitly a small chosen form rather than membership in an established tradition.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes of preparation, a suitable short gathering or segment, and a 15-minute review; defer if no fitting occasion is available.

### 1. Understand what participation means before joining

Identify the gathering's tradition or shared story, the purpose of its words and gestures, who leads, and which parts require belief, preparation or membership. Choose your honest role: eligible participant, respectful observer or decline. For the fallback, invite a willing companion to a ten-minute remembrance of a shared teacher, place or event: explain an opening acknowledgment, optional shared recollections, one quiet pause and a closing commitment to care. Both people choose the subject and can omit any part. Do not label this invented form as another tradition's rite.

Observable checks:

- The form has an identified tradition or honestly named shared story.
- Words gestures and leadership have an understood purpose.
- Eligibility and an honest role are established.
- Consent access and leaving are considered in advance.

### 2. Take part only in what you can sincerely undertake

Attend the suitable gathering or hold the agreed fallback. Follow visitor guidance, join only permitted and freely endorsed elements, and decline words or acts you cannot sincerely affirm. For the fallback, acknowledge why the shared subject matters, each offer an optional specific recollection, pause quietly and name one modest act of care before closing. Notice the other person's freedom and contribution. Do not photograph, record, evaluate devotion or turn the event into a test of someone else's belief. Record observer status honestly if you only watched.

Observable checks:

- An actual communal occasion takes place.
- Participation or observation is described accurately.
- Endorsed elements are undertaken sincerely.
- Other people retain privacy and freedom to decline.

### 3. Relate the experience to community and later conduct

Afterwards, explain how one actual element connected the individual to the community's story, commitments or sacred meaning. Distinguish what you understood, what you did and what you still question. Ask one respectful question through an appropriate willing person if clarification is useful. Within two days, carry out the modest act of care or responsibility you endorsed; if none was promised, choose one consistent with the form's meaning. Decide whether future participation fits your conscience and circumstances without ranking other pathways.

Observable checks:

- An experienced element is connected to shared meaning.
- Understanding action and remaining questions are distinguished.
- A related ordinary responsibility receives an attempt.
- Future participation remains a conscientious choice.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use an accessible online gathering if participation and privacy expectations are clear. The consented ten-minute fallback needs no travel, money, sacred language or disclosure of recent loss. With no willing companion or suitable group, prepare the map but leave communal participation untested; observing a recording is not a live shared occasion.

**Review.** What did you understand and sincerely join, how did it locate you in a shared story, and what freedom did you preserve for yourself and others?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** Two willing people used the plainly named remembrance, shared a specific story, honored silence and later carried out a modest care commitment.
- **Mixed:** A visitor understood the gathering better but chose to observe a restricted element and kept that limit explicit.
- **Contradictory:** Participation required a false profession of belief, a restricted rite was imitated without permission or another person's conscience was dismissed.
- **Inconclusive:** A video was watched alone and then recorded as evidence of taking part in a live communal practice.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.11 — Reason through authority conscience and a disputed practice

**Goal.** Distinguish the legitimate roles and limits of texts, teachers, institutions, inherited practice, experience and conscience, then give a principled process for agreement, dissent or reform.

**How this represents the competency.** The supplied low-stakes fictional case practices discernment. It neither adjudicates a real abuse allegation nor makes this app a religious, legal or safeguarding authority. A thoughtful decision is not measured by obedience or dissent alone.

**Before starting.** Use this fictional case: a community reading circle traditionally ends with a statement of gratitude. A respected teacher proposes requiring every visitor to say the same words, citing unity and a valued text. One visitor wants to remain silent honestly. The written visitor policy permits observation, and the coordinator can review meeting procedures. No real group needs to be contacted.

**Time and effort.** Three sessions of about 20 minutes, using the supplied case or a comparably safe ordinary disagreement.

### 1. Map the sources of authority without treating them as identical

Make six entries for the case: text, teacher, institution, inherited practice, experience and conscience. State what each contributes and what it cannot settle by itself. The text may guide gratitude but needs interpretation; the teacher may explain it but not rewrite the visitor policy alone; the coordinator manages procedure; tradition carries shared meaning; the visitor describes their own experience; conscience addresses sincere assent and conduct. Identify the exact disputed demand, who is affected and which facts are missing. Do not invent the text's wording to win the argument.

Observable checks:

- All six sources of authority have distinct roles and limits.
- The disputed demand and affected people are specific.
- Interpretation is separated from an actual rule.
- Missing facts are retained rather than invented.

### 2. Test legitimate direction and signs of coercion

Give the strongest fair reason for shared words and the strongest fair reason for voluntary silence. Examine the demand against its proper scope, the stated visitor policy, truthful assent, proportionality and an available appeal. Distinguish ordinary coordination, such as ending on time, from compelled belief or punishment for respectful refusal. Consider a counterfactual: if the coordinator threatens exclusion from essential support for silence, the issue exceeds a meeting preference and requires safety-conscious outside advice. Do not rehearse a confrontation with a threatening authority.

Observable checks:

- Both sides receive a fair reason.
- Scope consent and accountability are examined.
- Coordination is distinguished from compelled belief.
- A coercive escalation has an appropriately different response.

### 3. Give a principled process and test it against a changed case

Write a short proposed response: identify the text or policy to clarify, explain the conscientious concern, propose spoken words or respectful silence, and ask the coordinator to review the procedure through a fair route. State when you would comply, decline, seek independent advice or leave. Then change one fact: the group advertises a members-only rite and offers a separate open gathering. Re-evaluate without insisting that every form must be open to everyone. End with a reasoned disposition and an unresolved question, not a verdict that all tradition or all dissent is good.

Observable checks:

- A concrete clarification and review process is proposed.
- Compliance dissent and exit have stated conditions.
- The changed case produces a reasoned reassessment.
- A conclusion and remaining question preserve conscience and the rights of others.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use spoken reasoning, six labeled objects or the supplied case without personal disclosure. In an actual coercive or abusive setting, do not use this exercise to investigate, negotiate safety or confront an authority; use independent support appropriate to the circumstances. No complaint, confession or change of belief is required.

**Review.** Which authority was legitimate for which question, and what makes your process principled rather than convenient agreement or reflexive opposition?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The proposal preserved shared gratitude and honest silence, used the policy review route, and distinguished an open gathering from an appropriately restricted rite.
- **Mixed:** The reasoning recognized conscience but could not verify the cited text, so that interpretive question remained open.
- **Contradictory:** Respect for tradition became permission to coerce belief, or personal discomfort was treated as authority to erase every community boundary.
- **Inconclusive:** A preferred answer was announced without distinguishing authority roles, considering an objection or specifying a review process.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.12 — Cooperate across a real worldview difference without false agreement

**Goal.** Describe another tradition accurately from its own account, state a genuine disagreement without contempt and define shared conduct that does not require deceptive consensus.

**How this represents the competency.** The default compares two identified accounts, not every Christian or every humanist. A dialogue rehearsal tests understanding and cooperation design; it does not establish interfaith relationship skill until a real voluntary exchange occurs.

**Before starting.** Read the Vatican-hosted Nicene Creed and Humanists UK's Humanism introduction using the provided references. The first affirms a divine creator; the second presents a nonreligious account centered on reason and human welfare. Neither summary is a complete tradition. Use the fictional shared-library event below or a willing person's own account; never assign someone's beliefs from a group label.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes of reading, a 15-minute rehearsal or voluntary conversation and a 15-minute cooperation review.

### 1. State each account so its proponent could recognize it

Read both named accounts. For each, record one actual claim, its source location, one implication and one question the account does not answer for you. State the disagreement about a divine creator plainly; do not reduce it to different words for the same belief. Also identify a possible practical shared concern, such as care for neighbors, without pretending that its reasons must be identical. Correct your own summary against the source before evaluating it. With a willing conversation partner, ask them to correct their own view rather than speak for a whole tradition.

Observable checks:

- Two accounts are traced to their own sources.
- Each summary is checked for accuracy.
- A real disagreement is stated without caricature.
- Shared conduct is distinguished from shared reasons.

### 2. Practice a conversation that makes disagreement speakable

Use this fictional case: two volunteers disagree about belief in God while organizing a library event. One requests an optional prayer before setup; the other asks not to be included in prayer or described as endorsing it. Rehearse both positions in three turns each: a fair summary, a sincere disagreement and a curious clarifying question. Replace you people with this claim or my view. Give neither side a conversion goal. If using a real willing partner, agree time and topic in advance and stop if either person wants to end. Label rehearsal and live dialogue separately.

Observable checks:

- Both positions receive a fair summary and sincere disagreement.
- Questions invite clarification rather than a trap.
- No group is treated with contempt.
- Rehearsal or live dialogue is identified honestly.

### 3. Design cooperation that does not borrow another person's assent

For the library case, agree a shared practical task, optional prayer outside required work, truthful descriptions of who participates, and equal ability to help without worship. Identify any real venue rule that would need checking; do not invent permission. Test the arrangement against two changes: someone declines prayer, and someone asks to discuss belief during the task. State how each can decline without punishment. In a real opportunity, agree and carry out a comparably modest task; otherwise retain the result as a cooperation design and leave live cooperation untested.

Observable checks:

- A useful shared task and voluntary boundaries are specified.
- Participation is described without false endorsement.
- The arrangement handles two concrete disagreements.
- Designed cooperation is separated from any actual joint work.

**Adaptations and limits.** Read the sources with text to speech or use an accurate accessible edition. If access is missing, the supplied summaries support rehearsal only; source-accuracy checks remain unverified. No debate, religious disclosure, interfaith event or contact with an unwilling person is required. Unchosen participation is N/A, not deficient tolerance.

**Review.** What disagreement stayed real, what understanding improved, and exactly what could you do together without either person pretending agreement?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** Both sourced accounts were represented fairly and the library arrangement let people cooperate while prayer and assent remained voluntary; live cooperation was not claimed from rehearsal.
- **Mixed:** The disagreement was respectful, but an assumed venue rule still needed checking before the arrangement could be used.
- **Contradictory:** One person's silence was presented as assent, a tradition was caricatured or cooperation was made conditional on conversion.
- **Inconclusive:** Everyone was declared basically the same without examining a real claim, disagreement or workable shared task.

**Reference support:**

- [The Credo: Nicene Creed](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P13.HTM) — The published Nicene Creed affirms one divine creator. A specified religious confession, not empirical proof, a complete account of Christianity or evidence that every Christian expresses belief identically. It is compared without requiring assent.
- [Humanism](https://humanists.uk/humanism/) — Humanists UK describes humanism through a nonreligious naturalistic outlook, reason and concern for human welfare. A named organization's self-description, not a claim about every nonreligious person or a proof against religious belief. It supports comparison of stated positions, not validation of the dialogue exercise.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 03.13 — Interpret an intense experience with reason conduct and time

**Goal.** Separate a vivid experience from claims about its meaning and integrate it through careful reasoning, an appropriate outside perspective, ordinary conduct and later review.

**How this represents the competency.** Awe, unity, devotion, revelation and transcendence describe different experiences and interpretations. Intensity does not automatically establish truth or authority. This exercise interprets a manageable past or fictional event; it does not induce altered states or assess mental health.

**Before starting.** Use the supplied fictional case: after music and a striking sky, a person feels deeply connected to others and wonders whether they have been given a special mission. No unusual experience is required. A settled, non-distressing memory may replace the case if freely chosen. Do not seek substances, deprivation, hyperventilation, intense meditation or sensory overload.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes initially, one short perspective check and ordinary action, then a 15-minute review after several days.

### 1. Separate what happened from what it seems to mean

Make three headings: experienced, interpreted and proposed action. In the case, music, the sky and a feeling of connection belong under experienced; a special mission is an interpretation; quitting commitments would be a proposed action. Distinguish awe, a sense of unity, devotion and an apparent revelation rather than treating them as one proof. Write at least two possible accounts, such as personally meaningful emotion and a spiritual interpretation, and identify what the experience alone cannot decide. Include context such as tiredness only if known.

Observable checks:

- Experience interpretation and proposed action are separated.
- Different kinds of intense experience are distinguished.
- At least two possible accounts remain available.
- Unknown context and limits of inference are explicit.

### 2. Check an interpretation without outsourcing judgment

For a personal memory, discuss only what you choose with a trusted willing person who can question the interpretation without pressure; this might be a thoughtful peer or appropriate community member. Ask what would support the claim, what would count against it and whether the proposed action serves others responsibly. For the fictional case, compare two supplied responses: the feeling proves a unique mission, versus the feeling may matter while its explanation stays uncertain. Explain why reassurance, charisma or agreement alone does not settle the claim. No private disclosure or authority endorsement is required.

Observable checks:

- An outside perspective or explicitly fictional comparison is considered.
- Supporting and disconfirming reasons are requested.
- Authority and agreement are not treated as proof.
- Disclosure and participation remain voluntary.

### 3. Integrate through a reversible action and a later review

Choose one ordinary action consistent with the constructive meaning but reasonable under either interpretation: return something borrowed, keep an existing promise or offer a small welcome that is wanted. For the default case, try one such action in your own ordinary life without claiming the fictional experience as yours. After several days, compare the action's effects, the remaining meaning and any revised explanation. Maintain ordinary responsibilities and postpone major commitments based solely on the experience. Conclude what you can retain and what remains uncertain; an enduring feeling still does not prove a metaphysical claim.

Observable checks:

- A proportionate reversible action is actually attempted.
- Existing responsibilities are preserved.
- A later review examines conduct and interpretation separately.
- Retained meaning does not become automatic proof.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use the fictional case, spoken notes or a simple three-column diagram. Do not re-enter an upsetting state to obtain evidence. If an experience is frightening, disrupts ordinary functioning or seems to demand dangerous action, stop this educational exercise and seek suitable professional support; immediate danger needs urgent local help.

**Review.** What meaning survived patient examination, what changed in ordinary conduct, and what claim still lacks evidence?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The person retained a sense of connection, kept an ordinary promise, welcomed a questioning perspective and left the proposed special mission unproven after later review.
- **Mixed:** The small action was constructive, but the interpretation remained uncertain and the outside perspective was only a fictional comparison.
- **Contradictory:** An intense feeling became unquestionable authority for a major commitment, or a skeptical question was treated as evidence that outsiders were hostile.
- **Inconclusive:** The event was described vividly without separating interpretation, checking a perspective or reviewing any ordinary action over time.

**Reference support:**

- [Meditation and Mindfulness: Effectiveness and Safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety) — Meditation can involve negative experiences; health concerns should not be managed by replacing appropriate care with meditation. Supports a cautious stop-and-support boundary only. It does not establish safety or effectiveness of this product's short pause, classify a spiritual experience, or validate individual outcomes.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 04.01 — Name suffering honestly and choose one humane response

**Goal.** Respond to pain, injustice, tragedy or unanswered questions with truthful lament and a proportionate act of care, without requiring a tidy explanation before acting.

**How this represents the competency.** This is an exercise in honest language and humane response, not a treatment for distress or a solution to evil. Lament may be religious, secular, spoken, written or silent; no explanation of another person's suffering is imposed.

**Before starting.** Use this fictional case: a much-used neighborhood meeting place closes after storm damage, disrupting connection despite volunteers' sincere efforts. You may instead use a settled, manageable disappointment. Do not select an acute traumatic event, recent bereavement or someone else's private pain for the sake of producing evidence.

**Time and effort.** Three short sessions of up to 15 minutes, with pauses or deferral whenever useful.

### 1. Describe the hurt without correcting it into a lesson

Make four short entries: what happened; what is lost or unfair; what you feel or cannot yet name; and what remains unanswered. In the supplied case, separate physical damage from the loss of a shared place and from any question about why this happened. If prayer is part of your pathway, an unanswered prayer may be named without inventing a divine answer. Otherwise address the situation in ordinary language. Do not add at least, everything happens for a reason or an obligatory growth lesson to make the lament acceptable.

Observable checks:

- The event and its consequences are described concretely.
- Loss unfairness and unanswered questions are distinguished.
- Feeling or difficulty naming it is recorded honestly.
- No reassuring explanation is imposed.

### 2. Make room for a response without demanding agreement

Read the four entries privately or, if useful, share a limited version with a willing listener after asking whether they can listen without solving it. Use the request: I want this acknowledged; I am not asking you to explain why it happened. For the fictional case, rehearse an acknowledgment that names the lost place and effort, then contrast it with a response that minimizes the loss. Notice whether silence, anger, sorrow or uncertainty can remain without being judged as a failed performance.

Observable checks:

- An acknowledgment names the particular loss.
- Any listener freely agrees to the request.
- A minimizing response is distinguished from acknowledgment.
- The experience need not resolve into a preferred emotion.

### 3. Act on a need that does not depend on solving the question

Identify one ordinary need you can appropriately address: offer a wanted check-in, help someone find a permitted alternative meeting place, or take care of your own immediate rest or routine. In the fictional case, draft a practical notice separating confirmed closure information from unknown reopening dates; then make one comparable, modest act of care in ordinary life. Review what it helped and what it could not repair. Keep the unanswered question visible rather than calling the action an explanation of suffering.

Observable checks:

- A proportionate need and action are identified.
- An ordinary act of care is actually attempted.
- Help provided and loss remaining are separated.
- The unanswered question is not concealed by the action.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use a drawing, a few words or the fictional case. No public lament, theological agreement, painful disclosure or positive feeling is required. Stop if the topic becomes overwhelming; use support suited to your needs rather than pushing through for completion.

**Review.** What was acknowledged accurately, what care remained possible, and which question still deserves to remain unanswered?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The loss was named without a forced lesson, a wanted check-in occurred and uncertainty about the future remained explicit.
- **Mixed:** An honest lament was possible, but the offered help was not wanted and the participant respected that response.
- **Contradictory:** A listener insisted that suffering was deserved, secretly beneficial or evidence of insufficient faith.
- **Inconclusive:** A general statement about resilience replaced acknowledgment of the particular loss and no care action was attempted.

**Reference support:**

- [Grief after bereavement or loss](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/grief-bereavement-loss/) — Grief responses vary between people and can follow losses other than bereavement; support is available when coping is difficult. Supports acknowledging varied responses and appropriate support. It does not validate this educational exercise, prescribe a grief timetable or establish readiness for personal loss reflection.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 04.02 — Let finite time change one ordinary priority

**Goal.** Recognize impermanence in bodies, relationships, possessions and achievements, and translate that recognition into one concrete priority or appropriate reconciliation.

**How this represents the competency.** This considers finitude without predicting your death or assigning an expiry date to someone else. One changed priority is evidence of conduct, not proof of peace with mortality.

**Before starting.** Use a familiar object that has aged, a photograph you can comfortably view, or the supplied case of a favorite book whose pages are wearing out. No graphic material, illness scenario, cemetery visit or death countdown is needed. Keep the focus on an ordinary choice you can make safely.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes to reflect, one small action within a week and a 15-minute review.

### 1. Notice what changes and what cannot be kept indefinitely

Use four headings: body and capacity; relationships; possessions; achievements and roles. For each, name one ordinary way time changes it, and one thing you value that the change makes visible. The worn book may prompt preserving a note from its giver rather than buying more copies. Distinguish the fact that time is finite from a claim to know how much time remains. Include aging and loss without requiring yourself to imagine a frightening death or decide what follows it.

Observable checks:

- All four areas include a concrete form of change.
- A valued good is connected to the change.
- Finite time is separated from a predicted deadline.
- The reflection remains manageable and specific.

### 2. Choose a priority with an actual tradeoff

Pick one feasible change: spend twenty attentive minutes on a valued relationship, preserve a meaningful record, use an owned item well or make a small overdue repair. State what optional activity you will reduce to make room. If considering reconciliation, distinguish a safe invitation or acknowledgment from demanding contact or forgiveness; no unsafe relationship must be reopened. Use the default of preserving a short note about the book and making time to enjoy it. Decide when and how the action will happen.

Observable checks:

- A finite-time insight leads to a concrete priority.
- A real tradeoff creates room for the action.
- Any relational action respects safety and consent.
- A feasible time and method are specified.

### 3. Make the change and examine what it served

Carry out the chosen action and record the ordinary result. Did you actually give attention, preserve something, make a repair or offer a welcome without coercion? Compare the use of time with the optional activity displaced. Name what remains unfinished and what you can let remain so. Choose one sustainable continuation or conclude that the small change is enough. The aim is a more considered allocation of finite time, not urgency about maximizing every moment.

Observable checks:

- The chosen action is actually carried out.
- Its value is compared with the displaced activity.
- Unfinished matters are acknowledged without a total-life verdict.
- Any continuation remains sustainable.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use a neutral object, a supplied fictional example or spoken reflection. Rest may be the right priority. No bucket list, major purchase, medical forecast, farewell conversation or forced reconciliation is required. Defer if mortality reflection is destabilizing.

**Review.** Which specific choice changed because time is finite, and did it serve something you truly value?

- **Supportive:** The participant reduced an optional distraction, spent attentive time with a willing friend and kept one unfinished ambition in proportion.
- **Mixed:** The priority was sound, but the planned time was interrupted and a smaller attempt was honestly recorded.
- **Contradictory:** Finitude became panic to optimize every minute, pressure for forgiveness or a reason to neglect ordinary responsibilities.
- **Inconclusive:** A list of life goals was produced without changing an actual use of time or attention.

## 04.03 — Face a costly choice without inventing a lossless answer

**Goal.** Recognize that decent people can suffer, good efforts can fail and some choices preserve one good while losing another, then reason and act without denial or automatic nihilism.

**How this represents the competency.** A fictional low-stakes conflict develops tragic reasoning. It does not equip the participant to make emergency, clinical or life-and-death allocation decisions. Some losses can be reduced; that does not mean every conflict has a painless resolution.

**Before starting.** Use this case: a volunteer group has one remaining quiet room and the same two-hour slot requested by a study group and a bereavement-support group. Both followed the rules and neither caused the shortage. No safe extra room or different date is currently available. Your task is to reason about a fair process, not decide a real group's access.

**Time and effort.** Three sessions of about 20 minutes using the supplied case and one ordinary application.

### 1. Identify the real goods and the loss in every available option

Write what each group needs and why both claims matter. List three options: a transparent previously agreed rule, an agreed limited division if both activities can genuinely function, or a fair draw when no stronger relevant claim exists. For each, state who bears the cost and what remains unmet. Check the premises: if an accessible alternative room actually exists, the case changes. Do not invent one simply to avoid the hard part, blame either group for needing help, or assume that worthy effort guarantees a successful outcome.

Observable checks:

- Both legitimate goods are represented fairly.
- Each available option has an explicit residual loss.
- Relevant facts and hypothetical escape routes are distinguished.
- Need or failure is not converted into blame.

### 2. Choose a process you can justify to the disadvantaged side

Select a process for the stated facts, giving a reason connected to access, need, prior commitments or fair treatment. Explain it as if speaking to the group that loses the slot, without claiming they should be grateful. Name a mitigation that is genuinely available, such as first consideration at the next opening, and label it insufficient to replace the lost occasion. State who would have actual authority to decide and how a factual mistake could be corrected. Acknowledging moral remainder is part of the answer.

Observable checks:

- A decision process has a relevant reason.
- The explanation acknowledges the disadvantaged side.
- Mitigation does not erase the remaining loss.
- Authority and a route for factual correction are specified.

### 3. Apply the distinction between loss and meaninglessness

Choose a much smaller real conflict in your own time, such as two valued activities in one free hour. Decide, carry out the selected activity and acknowledge the other good left unsatisfied. Review whether disappointment led you to deny its value or declare all effort pointless. Return to the room case and state what good conduct still required even without a perfect outcome: honesty, fair process, care and practical mitigation. Keep the fictional high-consequence reasoning separate from your ordinary trial.

Observable checks:

- A small real conflict receives a decision and action.
- The unchosen good retains its acknowledged value.
- Disappointment is separated from total meaninglessness.
- The limits of transfer from the fictional case are explicit.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use drawings or spoken alternatives and a neutral scheduling conflict. No real vulnerable group should be involved to test your reasoning. If the case is personally distressing, use two recreational activities competing for one shared room instead.

**Review.** What loss could you reduce, what loss remained, and what still made responsible conduct possible?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The process was explained honestly, mitigation stayed proportionate and the ordinary trial preserved respect for the activity that could not be chosen.
- **Mixed:** A fair process was proposed, but an assumption about room access remained uncertain and needed checking.
- **Contradictory:** A nonexistent solution was invented to avoid acknowledging loss, or disappointment was treated as proof that goodness and effort are pointless.
- **Inconclusive:** The conflict was called difficult without identifying its goods, costs, authority or a defensible process.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.

## 04.04 — Find a small next orientation after a major disruption

**Goal.** Distinguish what a major loss has ended from what remains, identify practical and relational support, and choose one life-supporting commitment without requiring a redemptive story.

**How this represents the competency.** Bereavement, betrayal, disaster, illness, displacement and identity collapse differ in their demands. The default is fictional preparation, not evidence of recovery from catastrophe; personal use is limited to what feels manageable alongside appropriate support.

**Before starting.** Use this fictional case: a community workshop is destroyed by a storm. Nobody is injured, but a craftsperson loses tools, routines, a shared place and part of an identity built around making things. Immediate safety and shelter are already arranged. The exercise begins after urgent needs; it must never delay them. No personal disaster story is required.

**Time and effort.** Three sessions of up to 15 minutes, separated by rest; a single short entry or deferral is acceptable when capacity is low.

### 1. Separate what ended from what is still present

Make four small sections: ended, changed, remains and unknown. In the workshop case, the building and some tools are gone; routines and access have changed; knowledge, some relationships and the person's dignity remain; reopening is unknown. Include practical, relational and identity losses without calling remaining strengths compensation for everything lost. Name one thing that cannot currently be restored and one assumption you should not rush to settle. Do not require gratitude, forgiveness or a claim that the disaster happened for a reason.

Observable checks:

- Ended changed remaining and unknown elements are distinguished.
- Practical relational and identity effects are considered.
- An irretrievable loss is named without erasure.
- A premature conclusion is kept open.

### 2. Identify support by the need it can actually meet

Match each immediate nonemergency need to a realistic kind of support: information about the space, help with one practical task, companionship, financial or legal advice from a qualified source, or health support if needed. For the default, draft a bounded request to a trusted peer for a short conversation about accessible work space; do not send it as if the fictional loss were real. Specify what the helper is being asked to do and what remains outside that role. Include a second route if the first cannot help.

Observable checks:

- Different needs have appropriate support roles.
- One bounded request is specific and feasible.
- Helpers are not assigned authority beyond their role.
- A second route or unresolved support gap is recorded.

### 3. Try one life-supporting commitment without declaring recovery

Choose a small action that remains meaningful even if the former life cannot immediately return: maintain a basic routine, accept wanted company, care for an existing belonging or spend ten minutes on a familiar craft using available safe materials. In the fictional pathway, try one ordinary sustaining action yourself without claiming it proves recovery from loss. Later review effort, help and whether the action was tolerable or useful. Keep, reduce or stop it. A next step can coexist with grief, anger or uncertainty about identity.

Observable checks:

- A small sustaining commitment is freely chosen.
- One ordinary action is actually attempted.
- Its burden and support are reviewed.
- The conclusion does not equate action with recovery.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use the fictional case, four spoken words or a trusted helper who does not demand details. There is no schedule for rebuilding meaning. If the topic is overwhelming or basic needs are insecure, defer the exercise and prioritize suitable practical or professional help.

**Review.** What has ended, what remains available, who can help with which need, and what is one tolerable next commitment?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The map preserved the scale of the loss, identified a bounded request and supported one small routine without claiming the former identity was restored.
- **Mixed:** One action was attempted but cost too much effort, so the participant reduced it and retained the need for more support.
- **Contradictory:** Remaining strengths were used to minimize catastrophe or the person was pressured to find growth, forgive betrayal or rebuild on a deadline.
- **Inconclusive:** A hopeful future story was written without identifying what ended, a realistic support need or any tolerable action.

**Reference support:**

- [Grief after bereavement or loss](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/feelings-symptoms-behaviours/feelings-and-symptoms/grief-bereavement-loss/) — Grief responses vary between people and can follow losses other than bereavement; support is available when coping is difficult. Supports acknowledging varied responses and appropriate support. It does not validate this educational exercise, prescribe a grief timetable or establish readiness for personal loss reflection.

## 04.05 — Act on a chosen responsibility while a meaning question stays open

**Goal.** Examine a feeling of emptiness, repetition or arbitrariness and choose a defensible act of responsibility, care or faith without pretending that the existential question is settled.

**How this represents the competency.** An ordinary existential question is not a diagnosis. This exercise neither disproves meaninglessness nor treats depression; persistent distress or difficulty functioning calls for support beyond a philosophical task.

**Before starting.** Use the supplied thought: I keep doing the same household tasks; if they never stay finished, what is the point? Choose a neutral repetitive task such as returning reusable items to their places. No crisis, isolation, deprivation or dramatic life change is needed.

**Time and effort.** About 15 minutes of clarification, one 10-minute ordinary action and a short review on the following day.

### 1. Make the question precise enough to examine

Distinguish four claims: the task feels empty; it repeats; you cannot prove an ultimate purpose; and nothing you do can matter to anyone. Ask whether the first three actually establish the fourth. Name a concrete experience behind the thought and one thing still unknown. Consider both a secular chosen responsibility and a religious or philosophical commitment if genuinely yours; neither is required to resolve every question. Avoid arguing yourself into a preferred emotion or treating uncertainty as an intellectual failure.

Observable checks:

- Feeling repetition uncertainty and a total conclusion are separated.
- The question is tied to a concrete example.
- A questionable inference is examined.
- A genuine commitment or honest uncertainty is retained.

### 2. Choose an action with a local reason you can defend

For the default, return three shared or personal reusable items to their proper places so they are available when needed. State a local reason: preventing needless inconvenience is worthwhile to me even though the task will recur. If another small task better fits, identify its beneficiary and actual use before substituting. Spend no more than the planned ten minutes and record what you actually did. The action is an experiment in chosen responsibility, not a test that you must feel fulfilled afterward.

Observable checks:

- An ordinary action has a specific beneficiary or use.
- The reason does not require a final metaphysical answer.
- The action is actually carried out.
- Time and expectations remain proportionate.

### 3. Review meaning conduct and mood as different things

The next day, check the practical consequence: were the items available, did someone avoid extra effort, or is the use still unknown? Separately record whether the sense of emptiness changed. Decide whether the local reason remains one you endorse, even if the feeling persists. State the unresolved existential question accurately and choose repeat, another fitting action, rest or additional support. Do not turn one helpful act into a proof of universal meaning or one flat mood into proof that nothing matters.

Observable checks:

- A practical consequence is checked or left unknown.
- Mood and endorsed responsibility are reviewed separately.
- The unresolved question is stated honestly.
- The next step does not depend on a forced conclusion.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use a tiny accessible task, a seated sorting action, dictation or assistance by consent. If nothing feels manageable, defer rather than using activity as a moral test. Seek suitable support for persistent low mood, hopelessness or difficulty functioning; immediate danger needs urgent local help.

**Review.** What could you reasonably care for without certainty, and what did doing it answer or leave unanswered?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The small task reduced avoidable inconvenience while the larger meaning question remained open and was described more precisely.
- **Mixed:** The action had a useful effect but did not change the empty feeling, and the participant kept those outcomes separate.
- **Contradictory:** A person was told that busy work proves life meaningful or that lingering emptiness shows laziness or deficient faith.
- **Inconclusive:** The philosophical question was debated without specifying or attempting any locally justified action.

## 04.06 — Sustain humane care when improvement is not the assignment

**Goal.** Practice patience, lament, companionship, rest and faithful attention during a slow or unresolved season, without turning endurance into an obligation to optimize yourself.

**How this represents the competency.** The measure here is whether care and boundaries were honored, not whether the situation improved. Ordinary waiting provides a small practice; it does not demonstrate capacity to endure illness, deprivation or extreme suffering.

**Before starting.** Use a manageable unresolved situation such as waiting for an ordinary repair you cannot accelerate. Identify what essential tasks still need doing and what can wait. No one is asked to create hardship or remain in an unsafe situation for practice.

**Time and effort.** About 15 minutes to prepare and three short observations during a week; no extra workload or endurance challenge is added.

### 1. Define enough care for this period

Choose a deliberately small care floor: one ordinary need to protect, one necessary responsibility and one optional demand to reduce. Add room to name disappointment, accept suitable company and rest without earning it. For the repair case, protect a normal meal or break, keep the agreed appointment information available and pause a nonessential improvement project. Say what patience means here: avoiding needless pressure while taking useful action when information changes. It does not mean silence about harm or refusing help.

Observable checks:

- A small care floor and essential responsibility are defined.
- One optional demand is reduced.
- Lament companionship and rest have legitimate room.
- Patience is distinguished from enduring preventable harm.

### 2. Keep company with the situation without demanding a result

At three natural points during the week, notice whether the care floor was met, what help was available and what was difficult. One occasion may include a short wanted conversation, shared ordinary activity or quiet company. Ask for listening or practical help plainly rather than making another person fix the season. If alone, use a familiar gentle activity or a public contribution you value without pretending it is reciprocal companionship. Record missed care as information about need, not a failed character test.

Observable checks:

- Three ordinary occasions are reviewed without inventing success.
- Care and difficulty are recorded together.
- Any companionship respects the limits of the other person.
- Missed care leads to attention rather than self-punishment.

### 3. Review what should be carried and what should be put down

Compare the occasions for unnecessary burden, useful company, rest and responsibilities actually met. The unresolved repair may still be unresolved; do not call that a failed exercise. If conditions can be improved or left safely, consider that option rather than treating avoidable suffering as a badge of endurance.

Observable checks:

- Care and burden are reviewed without an improvement quota.
- A remaining need or help gap is identified.
- One commitment and one reduction are justified.
- Avoidable hardship is not made a virtue requirement.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use a single word per occasion, a reminder object or voluntary assistance. Rest, lament and accepting help are legitimate actions. There is no streak, forced gratitude, productivity target or requirement to tolerate mistreatment.

**Review.** What helped you remain humane while the situation stayed unresolved, and what demand can you reasonably stop carrying?

- **Mixed:** Some basic care was missed, revealing that the plan needed less burden and more support rather than a stricter routine.

- **Inconclusive:** A demanding improvement plan replaced attention to patience, company, lament or rest.

## 04.07 — Distinguish moral distress responsibility and the support needed

**Goal.** Recognize distress around acting, witnessing, failing to prevent or being betrayed in morally serious events, and distinguish guilt, shame, trauma, responsibility, possible repair and qualified support.

**How this represents the competency.** This is literacy and support preparation using a fictional case. Moral injury is not itself a diagnosis, and this exercise cannot diagnose PTSD, determine culpability or provide trauma treatment. It does not ask for a personal account of a morally injurious event.

**Before starting.** Use this fictional case: a volunteer coordinator made a decision with incomplete information, later learned someone lost access to needed help and feels both guilt about the choice and betrayed by withheld information. The case contains no details of violence. Read the linked National Center for PTSD explanation for terminology; no screening questionnaire or disclosure is required.

**Time and effort.** Three sessions of up to 15 minutes; pause or stop if the material is uncomfortable.

### 1. Separate feelings facts and judgments of the whole person

Make six brief entries. Guilt concerns an action or omission; shame can become a judgment of the whole self; trauma-related symptoms need qualified assessment; responsibility concerns what the person knew, could control and owed; repair concerns what may still help; support concerns who is qualified and safe to assist. Apply these distinctions to the fictional case without assigning a diagnosis or declaring innocence or guilt. Note the four possible relations to a morally serious event: acting, witnessing, not preventing and betrayal. Feelings alone do not establish the facts of responsibility.

Observable checks:

- All six distinctions are explained without diagnosis.
- Four possible relations to the event are recognized.
- Feelings and factual responsibility are separated.
- Missing knowledge and control are made explicit.

### 2. Prepare questions that need a qualified or trusted response

For the case, write four questions: what happened and what is still unknown; what responsibility was actually held; what repair could help without causing more harm; and what support is needed for continuing distress. Match needs to roles: a qualified mental health professional for symptoms, an appropriate independent adviser for institutional or legal responsibility, and a freely chosen trusted person or chaplain for companionship or meaning. Ask about confidentiality and its limits before sensitive disclosure. A friend cannot settle a diagnosis and a clinician does not automatically decide legal liability.

Observable checks:

- Questions separate facts responsibility repair and support.
- Support roles fit the questions they can address.
- Confidentiality limits are considered before disclosure.
- No helper is treated as an all-purpose authority.

### 3. Create a safe next-step route without forcing confession or repair

Locate one appropriate public support route or provider information page and note how an initial appointment or question could be requested. For the fictional case, draft a short request that names the type of help without private incident details. Identify why contacting an affected person, public confession or unsolicited restitution may be premature and requires care about their wishes and safety. Choose a low-risk interim step such as preserving ordinary care and gathering questions. Keep preparation distinct from treatment, actual repair or a decision about blame.

Observable checks:

- An appropriate support route is actually located.
- A minimal initial request protects private details.
- Premature confession or repair risks are recognized.
- Preparation is not recorded as treatment or absolution.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use the fictional case throughout, read with support or skip details. No personal narrative, symptom score, exposure exercise, apology, forgiveness or contact with an affected person is required. Persistent distress merits qualified help; immediate danger requires urgent local support.

**Review.** Which question belongs to facts, responsibility, care or qualified assessment, and what is the safest appropriate route for the next unanswered one?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The learner separated shame from a specific responsibility question, identified a suitable support route and drafted a private first inquiry without claiming a diagnosis or repair.
- **Mixed:** The distinctions were clear, but provider access or confidentiality remained uncertain and needed checking.
- **Contradictory:** A checklist assigned blame or a diagnosis, or the person was urged to confess publicly or contact someone harmed without regard for safety and consent.
- **Inconclusive:** Distress was labeled moral injury without distinguishing its questions or finding an appropriate support route.

**Reference support:**

- [Moral Injury and PTSD](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/understand/related/moral_injury_ptsd.asp) — Moral injury concerns distress after events violating deeply held values; it can involve guilt, shame or betrayal and coexist with PTSD, but is not itself a diagnosis. Supports terminology and qualified-support boundaries, not diagnosis, culpability judgments or a treatment protocol. No published therapy, questionnaire or symptom scoring is reproduced.

## 04.08 — Prepare care preferences and practice respectful accompaniment

**Goal.** Understand basic advance-care and comfort-care concepts, prepare questions about personal preferences and decision support, and practice presence that respects the dignity and choices of someone seriously ill.

**How this represents the competency.** This prepares a conversation; it creates no legally effective directive, treatment decision, prognosis or consent on another person's behalf. Advance-care requirements and clinical options need qualified local guidance. No dying person must participate in this exercise.

**Before starting.** Read the linked NIH MedlinePlus advance-care overview and NHS explanation of end-of-life and palliative care. Use the fictional case of an adult who wants understandable information, relief from discomfort and control over visitors. Keep any personal preferences in your own private document; the app needs only the observation checks.

**Time and effort.** About 25 minutes of reading and private preparation, a 15-minute conversation rehearsal and a short practical follow-through.

### 1. Prepare values and questions before choosing medical options

Explain the distinction between planning for future decisions and making a current treatment choice. The sources describe advance directives as legal documents, while palliative care concerns relief and support during serious illness; specific eligibility and services need local confirmation. Privately note what matters in care, whom you would want involved if you could not communicate, what that person would need to understand, and questions for a clinician about options and tradeoffs. - Comfort and support are included in the care questions.
- Values decision support and unknown preferences are identified.

If unfinished relationships arise, ask whether the person wants help communicating; do not organize reconciliation, spiritual counsel or visitors without consent. Medical questions and symptoms go to the care team.

Observable checks:

Do not assign someone legal authority merely by naming them. Review the fictional visit for dignity, grief and unwanted assumptions. Record when you will revisit your private notes and where they can be found by the appropriate people if formally completed. A rehearsal is not actual accompaniment, and a conversation note is not an executed directive.

Observable checks:

- One preparation step is actually completed.
- Any proposed support role is voluntary.
- Preferences and questions have a review point.
- Rehearsal and informal notes are not claimed as legal or clinical completion.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use the fictional case, accessible official information, interpretation support or a trusted helper. No disclosure of diagnoses, treatment choices, family conflict or existing documents is required. **Review.** What do you want a future conversation to clarify, and how would you accompany someone without taking over their choices or denying their situation?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The participant prepared values and clinician questions, found the proper local documentation route and rehearsed a visit that respected refusal and uncertainty.

- **Contradictory:** An informal note was treated as a valid directive, symptoms were managed without the care team or a visit imposed reassurance, reconciliation or belief.

## 04.09 — Hold a reason for hope alongside the reasons for grief

**Goal.** Develop realistic hope through relationship, remaining possibility, faith, meaning or action while acknowledging adverse evidence and outcomes you cannot promise.

Use two columns: reasons this outcome might improve, and reasons it might not. Mark facts, interpretations and unknowns in each. Name at least one kind of hope beyond winning the appeal, such as remaining connected or preserving knowledge, without claiming it replaces the lost place. Do not invent numerical odds; use them only if a trustworthy relevant source supplies them.

- Hopeful and adverse considerations are both concrete.
- Facts assumptions and unknowns are distinguished.
- A reason for grief is acknowledged.
- Outcome hope is separated from hope about response.

For the case, design a modest plan to preserve a shared planting note and ask permission before sharing it. - Its reason for hope is honestly stated.

### 3. Update hope when new information arrives

- New information changes the expectation when appropriate.
- The action is evaluated by its actual effect.
- Grief and any remaining hope can coexist.
- The conclusion avoids guaranteed or unfalsifiable reassurance.

## 04.10 — Continue responsibility when an important consequence cannot be undone

**Goal.** Distinguish irreversible consequence from remaining responsibility, make proportionate repair where possible and continue service or changed conduct without demanding that remorse erase the past.

**How this represents the competency.** This uses an ordinary fictional mistake, not a process for adjudicating serious wrongdoing or recovering from trauma. Repair can be incomplete, forgiveness belongs to others and personal growth does not cancel a consequence.

**Before starting.** Use this fictional case: someone carelessly discards a friend's handwritten note while tidying. The original cannot be recovered. An apology and a changed handling rule are possible, but a replacement would not be the same object. No participant needs to disclose a serious regret or contact a person harmed.

**Time and effort.** About 20 minutes to examine the case, one bounded responsibility action and a review several days later.

### 1. Separate the irreversible loss from what remains yours to do

Make three entries: cannot undo, can still address and do not control. In the note case, the original is gone; the person can acknowledge the mistake, ask whether any practical response is wanted and change how belongings are handled; they cannot require forgiveness or renewed trust. State the particular responsibility without turning it into a verdict that the whole person is worthless. Consider the related cases of a missed opportunity and incomplete restitution: regret may remain even after an appropriate next action.

Observable checks:

- Irreversible consequence and possible action are separated.
- A specific responsibility is acknowledged without a total worth verdict.
- Forgiveness and trust remain outside personal control.
- Incomplete restitution and missed opportunity are recognized.

### 2. Define repair that respects the affected person

Draft a brief acknowledgment for the fictional case: name the mistake and consequence, omit excuses, ask whether a practical response would be welcome and allow no response. Do not claim a replacement will restore the original. Add a prevention rule: do not discard another person's paper or objects without permission. For a real low-stakes application within your authority, put that handling rule into use or complete an already agreed small responsibility. Serious or unsafe contact requires appropriate independent advice rather than an exercise-driven apology.

Observable checks:

- The acknowledgment names the consequence without erasing it.
- Any proposed repair respects the wishes of the affected person.
- A prevention rule addresses the actual failure.
- A safe ordinary responsibility action is actually taken.

### 3. Review continued conduct without asking it to absolve the past

Several days later, check whether the rule or responsibility was followed at an actual opportunity; if none occurred, keep that part unverified. Distinguish remorse, practical learning and any remaining consequence. Choose a sustainable commitment to care or service that follows the value violated, such as careful treatment of shared belongings, without treating it as a purchase of forgiveness. Decide what must remain acknowledged and what repetitive self-punishment does not help repair. Support may be needed for continuing distress.

Observable checks:

- A later opportunity or its absence is recorded.
- Learning remorse and permanent consequence remain distinct.
- Continued care is connected to the violated value.
- Service is not treated as entitlement to forgiveness.

**Adaptations and limits.** Use only the fictional note, a spoken acknowledgment or an impersonal missed-opportunity case. No confession, apology delivery, self-punishment, reconciliation or forgiveness is required. If the topic is overwhelming or involves legal, trauma or safety concerns, use qualified support instead of escalating the exercise.

**Review.** What cannot be restored, what responsibility continues, and how can you live that responsibility without pretending either innocence or permanent worthlessness?

**What the outcomes can look like:**

- **Supportive:** The learner acknowledged the permanent loss, practiced a permission rule and reviewed a later opportunity without treating better conduct as restoration of the original.
- **Mixed:** The prevention rule was installed but had not yet faced a real opportunity, and the continuing consequence remained explicit.
- **Contradictory:** Good deeds were presented as buying forgiveness, a replacement was said to erase the loss or remorse became an obligation to punish oneself indefinitely.
- **Inconclusive:** Regret was expressed repeatedly without distinguishing consequences, proposing appropriate repair or taking any responsible action.

Minimum and full practice completion retain the existing action-count and explicit final-review rules. Neither means mastery of the competency.
